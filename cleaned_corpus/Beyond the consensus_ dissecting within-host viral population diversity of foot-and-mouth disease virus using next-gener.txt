23 Abstract
24 The sequence diversity of viral populations within individual hosts is the starting
25 material for selection and subsequent evolution of RNA viruses such as foot-­‐and-­‐mouth
26 disease virus (FMDV). Using next-­‐generation sequencing (NGS) performed on a Genome
27 Analyzer platform (Illumina), this study compared the viral populations within two
28 bovine epithelial samples (foot lesions) from a single animal with the Inoculum used to
29 initiate experimental infection. Genomic sequences were determined in duplicate
30 sequencing runs, and the consensus sequence determined by NGS, for the Inoculum,
31 was identical to that previously determined using the Sanger method. However, NGS
32 reveals the fine polymorphic sub-­‐structure of the viral population, from nucleotide
33 variants present at just below 50% frequency to those present at fractions of 1%. Some
34 of the higher frequency polymorphisms identified encoded changes within codons
35 associated with heparan sulphate binding and were present in both feet lesions
36 revealing intermediate stages in the evolution of a tissue-­‐culture adapted virus
37 replicating within a mammalian host. We identified 2,622, 1,434 and 1,703
38 polymorphisms in the Inoculum, and in the two foot lesions respectively: most of the
39 substitutions occurred only in a small fraction of the population and represent the
40 progeny from recent cellular replication prior to onset of any selective pressures. We
41 estimated an upper limit for the genome-­‐wide mutation rate of the virus within a cell to
42 be 7.8 x 10-­‐4 per nt. The greater depth of detection, achieved by NGS, demonstrates that
43 this method is a powerful and valuable tool for the dissection of FMDV populations
44 within-­‐hosts.
46 Introduction
47 RNA viruses evolve rapidly due to their large population size, high replication rate and
48 poor proof-­‐reading ability of their RNA-­‐dependent RNA polymerase. These viruses exist
49 as heterogeneous and complex populations comprising similar but non-­‐identical
50 genomes, but the evolutionary importance of this phenomenon remains unclear (15, 16,
51 25). Consensus sequencing identifies the predominant or major viral sequence present
52 in a sample, but is uninformative about minority variants that are present. Evidence for
53 population heterogeneity, where individual sequences differ from the consensus
54 sequence, has been routinely obtained using cloning approaches (1, 8), providing
55 insights into the evolutionary processes that shape viral populations. Unfortunately,
56 these cloning processes are laborious and usually provide only a limited resolution of
57 the mutant spectrum within a sample.
58 Next-­‐Generation Sequencing (NGS) techniques offer an unprecedented ‘step-­‐change’
59 increase in the amount of sequence data that can be generated from a sample. Albeit
60 mostly used for de-­‐novo sequencing of large genomes, NGS can be applied to re-­‐
61 sequence short viral genomes to obtain an ultra-­‐deep coverage. Therefore, NGS has the
62 potential to provide information beyond the consensus for a viral sample by revealing
63 nucleotide substitutions present in only a small fraction of the population. Several
64 studies have previously used the 454 pyrosequencing platform (Roche Applied Science)
65 to detect minority sequence variants for human viruses such as HIV-­‐1 (17, 22, 29, 36,
66 42, 45, 47), hepatitis B (32, 43), hepatitis C (48) and attenuated virus (46). A promising
67 alternative to 454, is reversible terminator-­‐based sequencing chemistry utilized by the
68 Illumina sequencing platform (Genome Analyzer II). The lower costs of the runs and the
69 higher throughput of this NGS approach are likely to make it widely used for deep-­‐
70 sequencing genomic investigations in the future (41). Illumina sequencing was recently
71 used to obtain sequences of West Nile Virus, through virus-­‐derived siRNA (3), mutant
72 viruses of severe acute respiratory syndrome (14), and human rhinovirus (7).
73 The aim of this study was to explore the extent to which the Illumina sequencing
74 platform can be used to characterize and monitor changes in viral sequence diversity
75 that occurs during replication of a positive-­‐stranded RNA virus within a host. This study
76 uses NGS to dissect foot-­‐and-­‐mouth disease virus (FMDV) within-­‐host population
77 structure, at a depth unobtainable by previous cloning techniques. Belonging to the
78 Picornaviridae family, FMDV is highly infectious causing vesicular lesions in the mouth
79 and on the feet of cloven-­‐hoofed animals. The samples analysed here were collected
80 during an infection experiment, in which a bovine host was inoculated with FMDV. We
81 developed a protocol that enabled identification of artifacts introduced during
82 amplification and sequencing which was used to validate and quantify the minority
83 sequence variants that were detected. In particular, we expected to see evidence for the
84 reversion of capsid amino acid residues responsible for heparan sulphate (HS) binding
85 associated with replication of a cell culture adapted strain of FMDV in a mammalian
86 host (20, 37). Although this study was conducted using FMDV, we anticipate that the
87 features we observe may be broadly representative of populations found in samples
88 obtained from other positive-­‐stranded RNA viruses.
89 Materials and Methods
90 Sample preparation and genome amplification
91 The samples analysed were collected during an infection experiment, in which a single
92 bovine host was inoculated intradermolingually with a dose of 105.7 50% tissue culture
93 infective doses (TCID ) of FMDV (O BFS 1860). The full-­‐length FMDV genome sequence
94 of this sample had been previously determined using Sanger sequencing (EU448369)
95 and was used as a reference genome in this study. The Inoculum was derived from a
96 bovine tongue vesicle specimen that had been passaged extensively in cell culture (9).
97 Total RNA (TRIzol, Invitrogen, Paisley, UK) was extracted from a sample of the Inoculum
98 as well as two 10% tissue suspensions prepared from epithelial lesions (front left foot
99 [FLF] and back right foot [BRF]) collected from the animal at 2 days post inoculation.
100 Reverse transcription was performed using an enzyme with high specificity
101 (Superscript III reverse transcriptase, Invitrogen), and an oligo-­‐dT primer (see Table 1).
102 For each sample, two PCR reactions generating long overlapping fragments (4557 bp
103 and 4317 bp respectively) were carried out using a proof-­‐reading enzyme mixture
104 (Platinum Taq Hi-­‐Fidelity, Invitrogen). For biosecurity reasons these individual
105 fragments together comprised <80% of the complete FMDV genome, and corresponded
106 to nts 351-­‐4909 and 3859-­‐8176 of EU448369. This enabled the amplified DNA to be
107 transported outside of the high containment FMD laboratory for sequencing at The Sir
108 Henry Wellcome Functional Genomics Facility (University of Glasgow). The samples
109 were amplified using the following cycling programme: 94 ˚C for 5 min, followed by 94
110 ˚C for 30 s, 55 ˚C for 30 s and 70 ˚C for 4 min, with a final step of 72 ˚C for 7 min. For each
111 RNA sample, the number of PCR cycles used was optimized (using parallel reactions
112 undertaken using Picogreen) such that products were collected from the exponential
113 part of the amplification curve prior to the plateau phase. Once established for each
114 sample, the same optimized cycle number was used for both runs. Individual PCR
115 products were visualized using agarose-­‐gel electrophoresis and quantified (Nanodrop,
116 Labtech), after which the concentrations of each PCR fragment were adjusted to
117 equimolar ratios for each of the three samples prior to sequence analysis. We repeated
118 the PCR of the original reverse-­‐transcribed sample in order to obtain an independent
119 replica of the amplified sample. The number of viral RNA copies put into the initial PCR
120 reaction was established by quantitative PCR for each of the samples (4).
121 Next Generation sequencing
122 Sequencing was carried out on the Genome Analyzer II platform (Illumina). Briefly, DNA
123 was fragmented using sonication and the resultant fragment distribution assessed by an
124 Agilent BioAnalyzer 2100. After size selection of fragments between 300 and 400 bp, a
125 library of purified genomic DNA was prepared by ligating adapters onto the fragment
126 ends to generate flow-­‐cell suitable templates. A unique 6-­‐nt sequence index, or ‘tag’ for
127 identification during analysis, was added to each sample by PCR. Once the
128 adapter/index modified fragments were pooled and attached to the flow-­‐cell by
129 complimentary surface-­‐bound primers, isothermal ‘bridging’ amplification formed
130 multiple DNA ‘clusters’ for reversible-­‐terminator sequencing, yielding reads of 50
131 nucleotides. We conducted two sequencing runs: in the first, we sequenced on a single
132 lane the three amplified viral populations (Inoculum, FLF and BRF) after tagging. The
133 second run was performed on a different flow cell: again, we sequenced the same
134 populations on a single lane, using a second, independent amplification of the three
135 original cDNAs. The second run was performed after the Illumina Genome Analyzer
136 went through an upgrade and was able to deliver longer reads of 70 nucleotides.
137 Data filtering
138 In order to make direct comparisons between the two runs, we trimmed reads from the
139 second run to 50nt. Typically, quality scores decreased along a read, as the reliability of
140 the sequencing process decreased with the number of cycles of the Sequencing
141 Platform. The second run yielded much better qualities following an upgrade of the
142 Illumina platform. For both runs, reads with average error per nt below a fixed
143 threshold (θ = 0.2%) were discarded to generate a flatter error profile along the read
144 (see Appendix and Figure A1). The first and last 5 nts of each aligned read were
145 removed from the analysis as they showed a higher number of mismatches to the
146 reference sequence due to insertions or deletions close to the edges of the reads (Figure
147 A2). More details can be found in Appendix.
148 Validation and analysis of sequence diversity in the samples
149 The frequency of site-­‐specific polymorphisms was estimated from the frequency of
150 mismatches of the aligned reads to the reference genome. A proportion of these
151 mismatches was expected to be artifactual, arising from a base mis-­‐calling in the
152 sequencing process, or from a PCR error in the amplification of the sample. In order to
153 identify polymorphisms arising from possible base mis-­‐calls in the sequencing reaction,
154 we used the quality score of each nucleotide read to compute the average probability of
155 a sequencing error, p, at each site i. Typical values of p are around 0.1%. Assuming
156 sequencing errors to be independent, we computed the expected number of such errors
157 as the mean of the binomial distribution B(x; p, n), where n is the coverage of site i. If
158 the observed number of mismatches exceeded this expected number of errors in both
159 runs then we excluded the possibility of a sequencing error. On the other hand, we
160 hypothesize that the probability that PCR errors in both runs independently generated
161 identical base changes at the same site is very low. Based on values quoted for the
162 enzymes used, we estimated that the error rate for the combined RT-­‐PCR amplification
163 process to be 7.7x10-­‐6 per base pair copied (2, 31, 38). We therefore defined
164 polymorphic sites that could not be attributed to sequencing errors and at which both
165 the most common and second most common nucleotides were the same between the
166 two runs to be ‘qualitatively validated sites’. For each site in the set of qualitatively
167 validated polymorphisms, we computed the 95% confidence intervals for the
168 polymorphism frequency using the binomial distribution above. If the 95% confidence
169 intervals from each run overlapped, we defined the polymorphism frequency estimates
170 from the two runs to be in quantitative agreement.
171 We assessed the quantitative repeatability of site-­‐specific polymorphism frequency
172 estimates by calculating Spearman Rank correlation coefficients between
173 polymorphism frequencies in the samples within each run and between polymorphism
174 frequencies from runs 1 and 2.

223 Results
224 In this section, we discuss the results of the Illumina sequencing of three FMDV
225 populations: the Inoculum (field sample O1/BFS1860/UK/67, used to artificially infect a
226 bovine host), and two lesions developed on two different feet of the host, 2 days after
227 inoculation.
228 Description and filtering of Illumina data
229 Sequences from the Illumina Genome Analyzer platform consist of a collection of several
230 million short reads. Sequencing was repeated following independent amplification of
231 cDNA generated through PCR. In the first run ~8% of the reads were discarded because
232 of unresolved nucleotides or corrupted tags. In the second run, ~3% of the reads were
233 discarded. Each nucleotide (nt) of each read is characterized by a quality score, which
234 quantifies the reliability of the base-­‐calling process during the sequencing. Only reads
235 whose average error per nt was below 0.2% (66% for the first run and 95% for the
236 second run) were considered for this analysis. Further details about the reads and the
237 filtering process can be found in the Appendix.
238 Coverage and consensus genomes
239 Reads that passed the quality test were aligned to the consensus genome sequence of
240 the starting material from which the Inoculum was prepared (see Appendix). The mean
241 coverage of the reference genome in the first run was 4863x for the Inoculum, 8665x for
242 the Front Left Foot (FLF) sample and 6594x for the Back Right Foot (BRF) respectively,
243 while for the second run it was 16827x for the Inoculum, 11924x for FLF and 15945x
244 for BRF (Figure 1A, B). For some samples (Inoculum and BRF, first run and FLF, second
245 run), the coverage for the two PCR fragments composing the viral genome was not
246 equal. More details on the statistics of the Illumina yield can be found in the Appendix.
247 We obtained consensus genomes for each sample, by identifying, site by site, the most
248 abundant nucleotide in the aligned reads. As expected, the consensus for the Inoculum
249 exactly matched the reference genome at all sites. For FLF, both runs indicated two
250 substitutions (nt 2767, G−>A, and nt 8140, G−>T). For BRF sample, the two runs
251 suggested slightly different consensus sequences: the first run revealed five
252 substitutions (nt 2767, G−>A, nt 3138, G−>A, nt 5138, T−>C, nt 7354, C−>T, nt 8134,
253 C−>T), whereas the second run had none. However, at position 8134 about 30% of the
254 reads in the second run showed a T in place of a C, and at position 2767 5% of the reads
255 had an A in place of a T. At the remaining 3 sites, the second run had a small number of
256 reads confirming the polymorphism found in the first run. This result indicates that the
257 same pattern of variation is present in both runs, although the frequency of the
258 mutations is not in quantitative agreement across the two runs for BRF. Finally, the
259 second run showed an almost-­‐consensus substitution in 49.9% of the reads (nt 2754 C-­‐
260 >T), which was present at a 10% frequency in the first run.
261 Validation of polymorphic sites
262 Mismatch frequencies, obtained by showing site by site the fraction of reads differing
263 from the consensus genome, are shown in Figure 2 (first run) and Figure 3 (second
264 run). An evident correlation is present between the regions of the sample genomes
265 receiving low coverage and those with the largest fraction of sites showing no variation
266 (Figs. 2A, second half, 2C, first half and 3B, first half). Using these raw data, and
267 considering only sites receiving coverage of 100x or more, we found polymorphisms at
268 7,755 sites in the Inoculum, 7,730 in FLF and 7,710 in BRF, out of the 7,825 nt
269 sequenced. While a few sites exhibited higher levels of polymorphism, the vast majority
270 of sites displayed a mismatch frequency around 0.1%.
271 After screening for possible PCR and sequencing artefacts, we found that qualitatively
272 validated polymorphisms were present at 2,622 sites for the Inoculum, 1,434 in FLF and
273 1,703 for BRF. The different consensus genomes obtained for BRF in the two runs can
274 be in part reconciled by noting that all six substitutions observed (nt 2754, 2767, 3138,
275 5138, 7354, 8134) are qualitatively validated in each run. We observed 2,469
276 quantitatively validated sites in the Inoculum (94% of qualitatively validated sites),
277 1,303 sites from the FLF (91% of qualitatively validated sites) and 1,528 sites (90% of
278 qualitatively validated sites) from the BRF
279 Site-­‐specific polymorphism (SSP) frequency at qualitatively validated sites was
280 correlated between the two runs for each of the three samples (Figure 4). The intra-­‐run
281 correlation for run 1 (Spearman Rank correlation: 0.64 [Inoc-­‐FLF], 0.55 [Inoc-­‐BRF] and
282 0.60 [FLF-­‐BRF]) was higher than run 2 (Spearman Rank correlation: 0.40 [Inoc-­‐FLF],
283 0.43 [Inoc-­‐BRF] and 0.42 [FLF-­‐BRF]). The reason for the poor intra-­‐run correlation for
284 run 2 is unclear. The number of viral RNA copies present in the initial PCR reactions was
285 found to be large (3.2x109 for the Inoculum, 6.4x108 for FLF and 2.4x108 for BRF):
286 assuming that the PCR process amplifies all genomes with the same probability, the
287 probability of resequencing the same genome is exceedingly low (<10-­‐5), thus excluding
288 the possibility of biases due to low viral load in the RNA. However, relative to run 1, run
289 2 yielded consistently lower amounts of DNA library concentrations per sample prior to
290 sequencing (3.4 vs 4.9 ng/µl, 3.7 vs 10.6 and 3.4 vs 9.5 ng/µl ng/µl for the inoculum, FLF
291 and BRF respectively): and this may have contributed to the differences between the
292 two consensus sequences. Despite the discrepancy in frequency of these mutations
293 between runs, the fact that the same mutations are present at the same qualitatively
294 validated sites in both runs provides confidence that the mutations are genuine and not
295 artifacts. The intra-­‐run correlation, together with the high fraction of quantitative
296 validation among the qualitatively validated SSPs provides sound evidence that nt
297 changes are linked between the different samples. Inter-­‐run correlation between the
298 samples (Spearman Rank correlation: 0.34 vs 0.44 and 0.50) indicates that validated
299 polymorphisms are unlikely to be artifacts.
300 Distribution of polymorphisms across the genome
301 There were 12 SSPs, whose average frequency between the two runs is above 1% in the
302 Inoculum, 19 in FLF, and 25 in BRF (see Supplementary Table). Some of these were
303 clustered in the capsid protein region (beginning of protein VP3) (1 in the Inoculum, 4
304 in FLF and 5 in BRF) and in the 3’ untranslated region (UTR) (6 in the Inoculum, 5 in
305 FLF and 6 in BRF). Where single reads spanning these sites within the VP3 or 3’UTR
306 were available, there was no evidence that that these mutations were linked together on
307 individual FMDV genomes. In particular, the first cluster was shared between the two
308 foot samples and corresponded to changes encoding amino acid residues associated
309 with heparan sulphate (HS) binding. The Inoculum used in this experiment had
310 undergone extensive cell culture passage and, in common with other in-­‐vitro adapted
311 viruses, utilizes HS as a cellular receptor (27). Subsequent replication in mammalian
312 hosts drives the reversion of positively charged amino acid residues at specific sites in
313 the viral capsid (20, 37). A consensus level substitution (>50%) exists within both feet
314 samples of run 1 compared to the reference sequence (see above and the
315 Supplementary Table). This polymorphism corresponded to a change within the 60th
316 codon of protein VP3 (VP360). Although below the level of the consensus sequence,
317 additional qualitatively validated SSPs that were present in both feet samples were
318 detected at four further sites (VP2134, two codon positions within VP356 and VP359) that
319 impact on the ability of FMDV to bind HS. All but one of the mutations clustered within
320 the 3’ UTR of the three samples were located within the first four RNA-­‐RNA pairings
321 either side of the apex of a conserved stem-­‐loop. This structure, one of two stem-­‐loops
322 previously predicted for FMDV and other picornaviruses (6) (33) is thought to generate
323 long-­‐distance RNA-­‐RNA interactions that may impact upon viral replication (40). The
324 presence of shared mutations between the two foot samples suggests a common history
325 for the viruses arising as a result of the shared route of intra-­‐host transmission from
326 initial replication sites in the tongue to epithelial sites in the feet via the blood.
327 However an alternative explanation – that the virus is subject to a common selection
328 pressure in both sites cannot be ruled out.
329 Frequency of site-­specific polymorphisms
330 Some variability was present almost everywhere on the genome. Above minimum
331 coverage of 100x, only 61 sites exhibited no polymorphism (0.79%) in the Inoculum, 59
332 (0.76%) in FLF and 49 (0.64%) in BRF. These sites received relatively low coverage,
333 suggesting that the absence of observed genetic variability may be due to lack of power
334 to detect it. By grouping the site-­‐specific polymorphism frequencies into discrete bins,
335 we can examine the proportion of sites experiencing different polymorphic frequencies
336 and thereby obtain a comprehensive picture of the heterogeneity in the viral
337 populations (Figure 5). Across the three samples, most sites exhibit a range of low-­‐
338 frequency SSPs between 0.01% -­‐ 1%. Only a few sites showed higher frequency
339 polymorphism, and these sites were more numerous for the samples from the feet than
340 from the Inoculum, indicating the generation of new high-­‐frequency substitutions
341 during the host passage. The dashed lines (Figure 5) correspond to the same analysis
342 restricted to qualitatively validated sites and reveal a similar pattern.
343 Statistics of polymorphic sites
344 NGS provided sufficient resolution to detect polymorphisms where two alternative
345 substitutions are simultaneously present. The secondary substitutions (the third most
346 abundant nucleotides in the reads at any particular site) that would have been
347 qualitatively valid even in the absence of the second most abundant nucleotide
348 substitution were present in 67 sites in the Inoculum, 15 in FLF and 41 in BRF.
349 Secondary substitutions typically appear at frequencies below 0.5%, confirming the
350 large amount of low-­‐frequency variability in the samples.
351 Table 2 shows that transversions are rare among the validated mutations, and thus κ
352 (defined as 2Ts/Tv) is high (however, similar values were reported in (8)). The ratio of
353 non-­‐synonymous to synonymous substitutions in the open reading frame, dN/dS, is
354 higher for FLF than for the other two samples because of the presence of the non-­‐
355 synonymous mutations in a large number of reads at positions 2754 and 2767,
356 associated with heparan sulphate binding amino acid reversions within VP356 and VP360
357 respectively. The mutation frequency at the third codon position is only marginally
358 higher than in the first and second positions. Taken together, these observations
359 suggest that the observed polymorphisms are dominated by mutations arising during
360 the last round of intra-­‐cellular replication and that have not been subject to extensive
361 purifying selection. Further evidence of this lack of selective pressure is provided by the
362 presence of validated polymorphisms generating STOP codons within the ORF. These
363 mutations are clearly lethal for the virus and would therefore be removed from the
364 population during infection of another cell: they must therefore have arisen during the
365 most recent rounds of viral replication. STOP codons were found at 24 sites in the
366 Inoculum, 9 sites in FLF and 21 sites in BRF, mostly at frequencies around 0.1% (with a
367 single exception in BRF where a mutation generating a STOP codon is present in 0.7% of
368 the reads).
369 The presence of STOP codons can be used to obtain an upper limit on the mutation rate
370 (per nucleotide per transcription event) of this virus. We hypothesise that these
371 mutations are lethal and are therefore generated in the last round of cellular replication.
372 Moreover, by assuming a replication strategy involving the minimum number of
373 copying events in the cell (the “stamping machine” strategy, see (44)), we obtain an
374 upper bound for the mutation rate of µ = 7.8 x 10-­‐4 per nucleotide per transcription
375 event (95% CI: 7.4 x 10-­‐4 – 8.3 x 10-­‐4), in line with previous estimates (e.g. (12, 13, 39)).
376 Finally, we can ask whether these results are broadly consistent with those acquired
377 from cloning studies. In ref. (8), Cottam et al. generated 26 viral capsid clones from an
378 FMDV sample taken from a single lesion of a bovine host. We simulated 10,000 sets of
379 26 viral capsid ‘clones’, essentially bootstrapping from the nucleotide frequencies
380 revealed by the NGS alignments to be present at each site within the capsid genes. Of
381 these 26 clones, the median number of sequences in each of the 10,000 simulated data
382 sets that were identical to the consensus was 12 (95% CI: 5-­‐17), compared to 15
383 observed in ref. (8). The median number of simulated clones containing 1, 2, 3, and 4
384 differences compared to the consensus were 9 (95% CI: 4-­‐14), 3 (95% CI 1-­‐7), 1, (95%
385 CI: 0-­‐3), and 0 (95% CI: 0-­‐1) respectively. These numbers correspond well with those
386 obtained by Cottam et al., (8) which were 6, 3, 2, and 0 respectively.
387 Complexity of the viral populations
388 In the host, the viral population evolves via extensive replication, mutation, and, at the
389 same time, selection. The result of these combined processes can be quantified by
390 computing how much ‘diversity’ is present within the three samples, using an entropy-­‐
391 like measure S that, site by site, takes a maximum value when all nts are present in the
392 same proportion. The entropy of the three populations, computed over the qualitatively
393 validated sites, shows higher values for the feet than for the Inoculum (S=0.01138 for
394 FLF, S=0.01198 for BRF and S=0.00841 for Inoculum), suggesting that repeated cycles of
395 cellular replication during passage in the host does result in greater viral population
396 diversity relative to the Inoculum.
398 Discussion
399 This study describes a novel use of Illumina NGS to investigate the population genetic
400 structure of a positive-­‐stranded RNA virus causing an acute-­‐acting disease in hosts.
401 These experiments generated an unprecedented amount of sequence data and required
402 a new systematic approach to confidently distinguish between sequences that were
403 actually present in the samples from artifacts introduced during the amplification and
404 sequencing steps of the sample processing. Results obtained here were consistent with
405 the findings of previous investigations, providing validation on the use of NGS in the
406 study of FMDV evolution within a host: Carrillo et al. (5) reports an average of 1-­‐5
407 substitutions per animal passage during an infection experiment in pigs, in line with the
408 2 substitutions we found in FLF. However, the case of the BRF points out a more
409 complex scenario that could not have been observed with consensus sequences only:
410 the drift of mutations above and below the threshold needed to appear in the
411 consensus. Apparent loss and subsequent regain of mutations during the transmission
412 of the infection across hosts (5) can be explained with this mechanism, which is made
413 more accessible to study by NGS. Moreover, the statistical characteristics of the SSPs we
414 identified (κ, dN/dS) are very similar to those found previously (8), further
415 corroborating the validity of our results. Finally, randomizations of the diversity
416 measured in the capsid region allowed us to obtain simulated clones whose
417 characteristics in terms of mutation were analogous to those found in (8). We conclude
418 that NGS data can be used to examine the nucleotide diversity of each genome position
419 at unprecedented resolution. Observing the mutant spectrum of the viral population at
420 a fine resolution will provide a more sophisticated understanding of evolutionary
421 processes shaping its variability.
422 Comparisons between the sequences recovered from the Inoculum and clinical lesions
423 provide new insights into the impact of early replication events on viral evolution
424 within a host. This study reveals that only a few sites displayed mutations present in a
425 large fraction of the population, i.e. high frequency polymorphisms (>1%), while the
426 vast majority of the polymorphisms were present at lower frequencies. We hypothesize
427 that the high frequency polymorphisms have been selected over multiple rounds of
428 replication within cells, and that the lower frequency polymorphisms most likely
429 directly reflect the high rate of mutation experienced by these viruses, as our estimate
430 of an upper limit of the genome-­‐wide mutation rate suggests. In this study we used a cell
431 culture adapted virus (as the Inoculum) which gave us the opportunity to monitor
432 changes at specific loci associated with the HS binding site that were under selection
433 pressure during initial replication in a mammalian host. Examination of these sites
434 (collated in the Supplementary Table) reveals for the first time the presence of
435 intermediate stages in the evolution of the viral population between a tissue culture
436 adapted genome and a host-­‐adapted genome.
437 Cordey et al. (7) investigate the dynamics of Human Rhinovirus (HRV) during an
438 infection experiment and in HeLa cells, and find results similar to ours in terms of
439 number of mutations fixed at the consensus level. However, while their approach
440 identifies hot and cold spots in the HRV ORF, and some minority variants, the resolution
441 is not sufficient to observe the micro-­‐evolutionary processes whose signature lies in
442 small fractions of the viral population (<2%). Moreover, their estimation of the
443 substitution rate during the infection is based solely on the count of the nucleotides
444 changed among those analyzed: although the value is compatible to our genome-­‐wide,
445 mutation rate, we believe that considering the cellular process of viral replication (and
446 specifically assuming the minimum number or copying events in a cell) allows us to gain
447 a better insight of the process generating variation in the viral population and obtain a
448 more stringent upper bound.
449 Figure 5 reveals that the viral population sequences are highly heterogeneous
450 supporting the findings of previous studies that have used cloning approaches (10, 28).
451 However, the massively increased coverage enabled by NGS enables the nature of this
452 heterogeneity to be established at much greater resolution. This is important for
453 understanding viral evolutionary processes because heterogeneity is a necessary but
454 not sufficient condition (23, 24) for the dominance of quasi-­‐species dynamics [see (10,
455 15) and references therein]. For quasi-­‐species dynamics to dominate the micro-­‐
456 evolutionary process, the frequency of a dominant sequence must be maintained
457 primarily by the back-­‐mutation or recombination of closely related genetic variants,
458 rather than the faithful replication of any single genome. This requires a balance of two
459 qualities: genetic variants closely related to the dominant sequence must be maintained
460 at sufficiently high prevalence; and that mutation and recombination rates must be
461 sufficiently high to generate the observed prevalence of the dominant sequence from
462 these variants. Previous studies have examined this question empirically and concluded
463 that these conditions are indeed met in many RNA viruses, mostly through studies of
464 mutational robustness as a selectable trait (“survival of the flattest” effect in which
465 selection acts not on the dominant sequence, but on the swarm of viruses immediately
466 mutationally adjacent to the dominant sequence) (11) (35) and reviewed in (19) with
467 particular focus on Hepatitis C virus. However, taking FMDV as an example, given that
468 there are ~25,000 one-­‐step mutant variants to any one sequence (3 alternative
469 nucleotides at each position of the ~8,300 nucleotide genome), NGS approaches are
470 clearly a powerful tool for examining directly whether viral populations are structured
471 in a way that is consistent with a quasi-­‐species dynamic.
472 NGS data can be coupled to evolutionary models to estimate parameters, such as the
473 genome-­‐wide mutation rate of FMDV. Here, we computed this number hypothesizing
474 that the viral replication strategy followed the so-­‐called “stamping machine” mode of
475 replication, where all viral genomes leaving the cells are obtained as copies of “first
476 generation” negative stranded genomes, which are in turn direct copies of the genomic
477 RNA originally infecting the cells. For this reason, the estimate of 7.8x10-­‐4 per genome
478 per duplication round should be considered an upper bound on the mutation rate which
479 is a tighter estimate than previous figures obtained for other RNA viruses (12, 13) as a
480 result of the deep coverage that NGS generates. Were the replication strategy
481 “geometric” (i.e. including the possibility of several rounds of positive/negative strand
482 copying before exiting the cell), the mutation rate would be several-­‐fold (perhaps 3-­‐6
483 times) lower (44). The assumptions that all nucleotide mutations at a site are equally
484 likely, and that all STOP codons are generated by a de novo mutation are also likely lead
485 to an overestimation of the mutation rate.
486 To present date, the analysis of the amount of complexity carried by a genome has
487 mostly coincided with information-­‐theoretical measures, aimed to quantify at the
488 entropy and the frequency distributions of short oligomers (26, 30). This approach
489 looks at the “horizontal” complexity along a genome; with NGS we are now able to
490 obtain the closely-­‐related sequences for a whole viral population in a single experiment,
491 thus enabling us to look at the “vertical” complexity of the viral variants, i.e. at the
492 amount of variability present in the population at each site.
493 A viral population within a host undergoes complex processes, including the onset of
494 infection, cellular replication, selection, and migration to different tissues. In particular,
495 it is not clear how the diversity generated within a cell propagates through a host to
496 give rise to the amount of diversity we observe. The data collected in studies like this
497 can be used for building models aimed at understanding the link between the micro-­‐
498 evolution of FMDV at the cellular scale with the population heterogeneity at the host
499 scale. We anticipate that a model of viral replication across several cell generations
500 within a host will produce a more stringent upper bound to the genome-­‐wide mutation
501 rate.
502 Although further work is required, these findings strongly suggest that data generated
503 through the use of this methodology can provide novel insights into viral evolutionary
504 dynamics at a greater resolution than previously achieved for a positive-­‐stranded virus
505 such as FMDV. In particular, the genome wide assessment of polymorphic frequencies is
506 likely to be an important asset in the parameterization of models that can evaluate the
507 role of quasi-­‐species dynamics in RNA virus evolution.

521 Appendix: Statistics and treatment of Illumina sequences
522 Basic statistics of Illumina yield
523 The reads obtained with the Illumina Genome Analyzer were collected in .fastq files.
524 The first run consisted of a total of 7,190,884 reads of 57-­‐nucleotides (nt) in length. The
525 last 7 nts of each read defined the sequence tag, and were used to assign individual
526 reads to each sample. Reads containing at least one unresolved nt (387,809, 5.55% of
527 the total), and reads having a corrupted tag (207,749, 2.89% of the total) were removed
528 from the analysis. The 6,595,326 remaining, 50nt-­‐long reads, were assigned to the three
529 samples: 1,723,151 (26.1%) had the first tag (corresponding to the Inoculum),
530 2,751,260 (41.7%) had the second tag (lesion on the Front Left Foot, or FLF) and
531 2,112,932 (32.0%) had the third tag (lesion on the Back Right Foot, or BRF).
532 The second run yielded 10,116,147 79-­‐nt long reads, with the last 9 nts containing the
533 sequence tag. 26,428 (0.27%) reads contained at least one unresolved nt and 288,230
534 (2.85%) reads had a corrupted tag and were removed from the analysis. Among the
535 remaining 9,801,489 70-­‐nt reads, 3,775,685 (38.5%) belonged to the Inoculum,
536 2,542,913 (25.9%) to FLF and 3,482,891 (35.5%) to BRF.
538 Data filtering
539 The quality scores associated with each nucleotide were lower on the first run and
540 decreased towards the end of reads (Figure A1). In order to make direct comparisons
541 between the two runs, we trimmed reads from the second run to 50nt. Typically, quality
542 scores decreased along a read, as the reliability of the sequencing process decreased
543 with the number of cycles of the Sequencing Platform. As Figure A1 shows, a trade off is
544 present between the number of reads kept and their quality. For both runs, we
545 discarded reads with average error per nt below θ = 0.2%, resulting in a flatter error
546 profile along the read.
547 With this choice of the threshold, 66% of the reads were retained from the first run (a
548 total of 4,361,101 reads: 1,060,906 for the Inoculum, 1,736,381 for FLF and 1,328,588
549 for BRF), and 95% of reads from the second run (a total of 9,277,876 reads: 3,567,541
550 for the Inoculum, 2,412,897 for FLF and 3,303,438 for BRF). The better performance of
551 the second dataset has to be attributed to an upgrade of the Illumina platform.
553 Reads alignment and trimming
554 The vast majority of the filtered 50nt-­‐reads aligned unambiguously with less than 5
555 mismatches to the reference inoculum genome, previously established using
556 conventional Sanger sequencing (9) (Genbank accession no. EU448369), (run 1: 92.5%
557 for Inoculum, 98.9% for FLF, and 97.8% for BRF, run 2: 95.8% for Inoculum, 98.4% for
558 FLF and 96.2% for BRF). The remaining reads were either ambiguously aligned reads or
559 contained a large number (>4) of mismatches to the reference sequence, and were
560 discarded from the analysis. For each sample, an almost equal number of reads were
561 derived from positive and negative strands of the viral cDNA.
562 Further filtering of the data was undertaken after alignment of the reads. Within the
563 aligned reads, mismatches occurred with similar frequency at each of the 50nt of the
564 reads, except from the edges, where a higher number of mismatches was observed
565 (Figure A2). Presumably, these peaks were due to a small number of sequences with
566 insertions or deletions close to the ends of the reads: for subsequent analysis we
567 trimmed away the first and last 5 nts of each aligned read, leaving only the 40 central
568 nucleotides where the mismatch curve was flat.
