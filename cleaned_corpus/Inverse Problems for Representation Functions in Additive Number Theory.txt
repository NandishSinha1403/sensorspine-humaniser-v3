A central concept in additive number theory is basis. Let S be a subset of X.
The set A is called
(1) a basis of order h for S if S ⊆ hA, that is, if every element of S can be
represented as the sum of h not necessarily distinct elements of A,
(2) an asymptotic basis of order h for S if S \hA is finite, that is, if all but
finitely many elements of S can be represented as the sum of h not neces-
sarily distinct elements of A.
For example, the nonnegative cubes are a basis of order 9 for N (Wieferich’s
theorem), an asymptotic basis of order 7 for N (Linnik’s theorem), and a basis of
order 4 for almost all N (Davenport’s theorem). A large part of classical additive
number theory is the study of how special sets of integers (for example, the k-th
powers,polygonalnumbers,and primes)are bases for the nonnegativeintegers (cf.
Nathanson [13]).
Our definition of basis is weak in the sense that, if X is an abelian semigroup
withadditiveidentity0,theneverysubsetofXhasabasisoforderhforallh≥1..

if S is any subset of X, then S ⊆hX. We shall call the subset A of X
(1) an exact basis of order h for S if S = hA, that is, if the elements of S are
precisely the elements of X that can be represented as the sum of h not
necessarily distinct elements of A,
(2) an exact asymptotic basis of order h for S if hA⊆S and S\hA is finite.
