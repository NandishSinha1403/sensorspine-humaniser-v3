28 Abstract
29 Background
30 The domestication and subsequent selection by humans to create breeds and biological types of
31 cattle undoubtedly altered the patterning of variation within their genomes. Strong selection to
32 fix advantageous large-effect mutations underlying domesticability, breed characteristics or
33 productivity created selective sweeps in which variation was lost in the chromosomal region
34 flanking the selected allele. Selective sweeps have now been identified in the genomes of many
35 animal species including humans, dogs, horses, and chickens. Here, we attempt to identify and
36 characterize regions of the bovine genome that have been subjected to selective sweeps.
37 Results
38 Two datasets were used for the discovery and validation of selective sweeps via the fixation of
39 alleles at a series of contiguous SNP loci. BovineSNP50 data were used to identify 28 putative
40 sweep regions among 14 diverse cattle breeds. To validate these regions, Affymetrix BOS 1
41 prescreening assay data for five breeds were used to identify 114 regions and validate 5 regions
42 identified using the BovineSNP50 data. Many genes are located within these regions and the lack
43 of sequence data for the analyzed breeds precludes the nomination of selected genes or variants
44 and limits the prediction of the selected phenotypes. However, phenotypes that we predict to
45 have historically been under strong selection include horned-polled, coat color, stature, ear
46 morphology, and behavior.
47 Conclusions
48 The identified selective sweeps represent recent events associated with breed formation rather
49 than ancient events associated with domestication. No sweep regions were shared between
50 indicine and taurine breeds reflecting their divergent selection histories and environmental
51 habitats to which these sub-species have adapted. While some sweep regions have previously
52 been identified using different methods, a primary finding of this study is the sensitivity of
53 results to assay resolution. Despite the bias towards common SNPs in the BovineSNP50 design,
54 false positive sweep regions appear to be common due to the limited resolution of the assay.
55 Furthermore, this assay design bias leads to the majority of detected sweep regions being breed
56 specific, or limited to only a small number of breeds, restricting the suite of selected phenotypes
57 that can be detected to primarily those associated with breed characteristics.

63 Background
64 The transition from hunter-gather lifestyles to permanent dwelling societies was facilitated by
65 both plant and animal domestication [1]. The domestication of cattle occurred between 8,000 and
66 10,000 years ago and led to changes in the genome of the species due to the effects of
67 demography and selection [2, 3]. Much of the variation within the genetically diverse ancestral
68 population was either lost due to the limited sampling of animals within the sites of
69 domestication or was partitioned into the subpopulations which went on to become recognized as
70 distinct breeds. Selection for the phenotypes contributing to domesticability, biological type
71 (draft, milk, meat) and the aesthetically appealing morphologies that have become breed
72 hallmarks (polled, coat color and patterning [4-8]) have also impacted the extent and distribution
73 of variability within the genome.
74 Strong on-going selection for variants of large effect leads to a loss of variation within
75 the chromosomal region flanking the selected variant and ultimately the complete fixation of the
76 haplotype which harbors the variant. This phenomenon is known as the “hitch-hiking effect” [9]
77 and the regions of the genome in which selection has driven a haplotype to complete fixation are
78 defined as having been subjected to “selective sweeps.” Studies of selective sweeps differ from
79 the classical forward genetic approach which progresses from a phenotype to the identification of
80 the underlying causal genes or mutations. Rather, they follow a reverse genetics approach that
81 begins with the signature of selection and attempts to infer the selected mutation and its
82 associated phenotype [1].
83 Several approaches have been used to identify regions of the genome which have been
84 subjected to selective sweeps, including those based on allele frequency spectra, linkage
85 disequilibrium, haplotype structure, and employing sophisticated models fit using, for example,
86 maximum composite likelihood estimation [10-12]. These approaches require the use of high-
87 density single nucleotide polymorphism (SNP) data which has previously been shown to be
88 useful for detecting selective sweeps in human populations [13, 14]. Studies aimed at localizing
89 selective sweeps have been performed in many animal species using SNP and microsatellite loci.
90 In chicken, selective sweeps have been found to involve loci believed to be involved in
91 domestication and include BCDO2 which controls yellow and white skin colors, SEMA3A which
92 plays a role in axonal pathfinding important in brain development, and THSR which is postulated
93 to derestrict the regulation of seasonal reproduction [15]. Selective sweeps have been found in
94 the dog genome at TRYP1 which controls black coat color in Large Munsterlanders and in
95 FGFR3 in Daschshunds [16]. FGFR3 mutations cause achondroplasia in humans and cattle.
96 Other studies in dogs have identified a sweep surrounding IGF1 which is responsible for size
97 variation [17] and in a region for which the selected phenotype is unknown in Boxers [18]. These
98 sweeps range in size from 28 kb to 40 Mb suggesting considerable variation in the intensity of
99 selection and also in the population census size. A ~75 kb selective sweep at a locus influencing
100 stature has also been found in the horse genome in a region upstream of a transcription factor
101 (LCORL) that is associated with variation in human height [19]. A 28 kb selective sweep in a
102 region of the swine genome harboring IGF2 has also been found for which the underlying
103 selected phenotypes are increased muscle mass and decreased fat deposition [20]. Domestic
104 animals have been demonstrated to be excellent models for genetic studies due to the availability
105 of extensive pedigrees and because, as species, they are frequently more genetically diverse than
106 humans [21]. However, relatively few selective sweep studies have been conducted to date in
107 livestock species to elucidate the genes which have been selected by humans to create the diverse
108 spectrum of breeds and specialized biological types for draft, milk and meat production.
109 In this study, we sought to identify the signatures of selective sweeps in cattle as genomic
110 regions in which contiguous SNPs from the BovineSNP50 assay were all individually fixed for a
111 single allele within a sample of 6,373 genotyped individuals representing 14 breeds (Table 1).
112 Our goals are ultimately to identify the selected mutations and phenotypes which were selected
113 by our ancestral herdsmen in the processes of domestication and formation of breeds and
114 biological types.
115 Results
116 Regions identified as harboring selective sweeps using BovineSNP50 data
117 Twenty eight genomic regions on 15 chromosomes were identified as putatively harboring
118 selective sweeps (Table 2). Selective sweeps were found in all 14 breeds; however, breed-
119 specific selective sweeps were not identified in every breed. Twenty three predicted sweeps were
120 breed-specific and 5 were shared among two to seven breeds with 4 of those containing three or
121 more (Figure 1). Breed-specific sweeps averaged 336,263 bp and ranged in size from 207,050 to
122 702,424 bp whereas sweeps common to two or more breeds averaged 441,314 bp and ranged in
123 size from 214,554 to 866,260 bp. The haplotypes found at the core loci involved in each of these
124 putative selective sweeps were identical for each of the breeds in which the sweep was detected.
125 Three of the five selective sweep regions detected in two or more breeds involved both
126 beef and dairy breeds, whereas the 358 kb region on BTA12 is common to only the Angus,
127 Salers, Shorthorn and Simmental beef breeds. None of the five selective sweeps shared by two or
128 more breeds are phylogenetically congruent in the sense that we might have expected the sweep
129 to have arisen in a recent common ancestor [22]. While the large selective sweep region on
130 chromosome 6 at ~75.9-76.7 Mb is shared by the closely related Salers and Brown Swiss breeds,
131 Salers and Limousin are sister breeds [2, 3] and Limousin does not demonstrate evidence of this
132 sweep. There were no putative sweeps shared in common between any of the taurine breeds with
133 the indicine Brahman breed. The DAVID functional analysis did not yield any significant
134 functional enrichment of gene ontology terms for these sweep regions.
135 Validation of selective sweep regions using Affymetrix prescreening assay data
136 To validate the selective sweep regions identified using the BovineSNP50 data, we examined
137 ultra high-density SNP genotype data produced by the Affymetrix prescreening assay used for
138 the design of the BOS 1 array (AFFXB1P) for small samples from five breeds, of which four
139 were included in our BovineSNP50 data (Table 1). Since only a subset of the 14 breeds
140 genotyped with the BovineSNP50 assay were also assayed with the AFFXB1P assay, we had the
141 potential to validate only 11 of the putative selective sweep regions identified in Table 2 and five
142 regions were confirmed (Table 3). Only two of the regions were confirmed in the same breeds
143 that led to their identification using the BovineSNP50 assay and for two of the remaining
144 regions, discovery occurred in Hanwoo and confirmation occurred in the phylogenetically
145 similar Wagyu breed [2, 3]. However, the region on BTA13 was identified in Holstein using the
146 BovineSNP50 data but was independently validated in Wagyu by the AFFXB1P data.
147 Selective sweep regions independently identified using the AFFXB1P data
148 A total of 86 putative selective sweep regions spanning from 200,088 to 846,420 bp and
149 averaging 321,001 bp were identified on 28 of the 29 bovine autosomes in the genotyped 5
150 breeds and, of these regions, 20 were shared in two or more breeds (Tables 4 and 5). These
151 regions harbored from 20 to 477 contiguous SNPs with no more than 5% of the SNPs being
152 variable. Among the selective sweeps identified in two or more breeds, the number of breeds
153 included in this analysis was too small to make inferences about the phylogenetic congruence of
154 shared sweep regions, however, three sweeps were found only in the closely related East Asian
155 Wagyu and Hanwoo breeds. All of the breeds that shared a common selective sweep (Table 5)
156 were fixed for the same core haplotype with two exceptions. The sweep on BTA16 at
157 45,386,065-45,652,672 and on BTA21 at 1,727,412-2,142,823, both shared by the Angus and
158 Simmental breeds, were fixed for haplotypes that differed at a single SNP. The allele that varied
159 in the haplotypes that were swept to fixation on BTA16 was the 7th of 248 SNPs, whereas on
160 BTA21 the variable allele was at the 22nd of 30 SNPs, indicating a conserved core at both loci.
161 No sweeps were found in common between the four taurine breeds and the indicine Brahman.
162 Discussion
163 The BovineSNP50 data identified a sweep region towards the centromere of BTA1 harboring 11
164 contiguous monomorphic SNPs and spanning 301 kb in Angus (Figure 2). This region contains
165 the polled locus [6] for which this breed has been strongly selected for homozygosity of the
166 polled allele [23]. Hereford cattle are also homozygous for the dominant spotted allele at the
167 spotted locus which is a candidate for the 210 kb sweep region at 70.65-70.87 Mb on BTA6 [8].
168 The spotted locus affects the white points on the face, underline, feet and tail which are a
169 definitive characteristic of the Hereford breed. These breed-specific sweeps are clearly examples
170 of strong selection on loci which underlie phenotypes that are hallmarks of certain breeds and
171 where the underlying causal mutation is known or has been mapped to a chromosomal location.
172 Several regions in which we detected putative sweeps using the BovineSNP50 data
173 contain no annotated genes and either this reflects the incomplete annotation of the bovine
174 genome, or the fact that the selected functional mutation within each of these regions is not
175 located within a protein coding gene. Examining each of these regions for the alignment of
176 human, pig and sheep mRNA orthologs failed to identify any. Recent work has identified
177 ncRNAs which regulate the expression of nearby genes [24] and may help identify candidates for
178 the mutations in these regions that were subjected to selection. The putative sweep shared among
179 six breeds on BTA24 contains four pseudogenes, of which three are olfactory receptor-like.
180 Whether any of these pseudogenes are expressed is unclear; however, olfactory receptor loci
181 were detected as being recently duplicated within the bovine genome [25] suggesting that they
182 may also be under strong selection for newly evolving functions. The common sweep region on
183 BTA12 contains neurobeachin (NBEA) and mab21-like 1 (MAB21L1) which have been
184 implicated in human autism and psychiatric disorders, respectively [26-28]. Since these
185 phenotypes represent extreme behaviors, it is intriguing to speculate that mutations in these
186 genes may also predispose cattle to increased docility and more favorable temperaments when
187 handled by man.
188 A selective sweep on BTA13 was detected in Holstein using the BovineSNP50 data and
189 was also discovered in Wagyu using the AFFXB1P data. The region from 15.49-15.74 Mb
190 contains diacylglycerol kinase zeta (DGKZ) and several bovine ESTs. DGKZ has been
191 implicated as a member of the downstream leptin signaling pathway and reduced expression or
192 activity within the hypothalamus has been associated with obesity [29]. The Holstein and Wagyu
193 breeds are phylogenetically distant, however, Wagyu are believed to have been influenced by
194 several European taurine breeds, including Holstein, during the late 1800s and both breeds are
195 known for their ability to store intramuscular fat without accumulating excessive subcutaneous
196 fat. The 351 kb selective sweep region from 1.67-2.02 Mb on BTA1 found in Angus using the
197 BovineSNP50 and validated in Angus using the AFFXB1P data contains a fixed 321 marker
198 haplotype which harbors the polled locus [6] a hallmark of the breed which contains only polled
199 animals.
200 A region on BTA18 from 14.72-14.97 Mb was detected to harbor a selective sweep in
201 Hanwoo cattle using the BovineSNP50 data and rediscovered in Angus and Simmental, using the
202 AFFXB1P data. This 248 kb region contains several annotated genes (Table 3), but importantly
203 harbors melanocortin 1 receptor (MC1R) in which mutations lead to the black coat color in cattle
204 [7]. Angus have been strongly selected for black coat color and almost all registered animals are
205 now homozygous black confirming that the basis for this selective sweep in Angus was for the
206 black coat color allele. The American Simmental Association registers crossbred animals and
207 many breeders have developed Simmental × Angus crossbreds to capitalize on the premium that
208 carcasses from black coated cattle can achieve if they qualify for Angus branded products. While
209 all of the Simmentals genotyped with the BovineSNP50 assay were purebred, of the 6 Simmental
210 animals genotyped with the AFFXB1P assay, one was homozygous black, four were
211 heterozygous and one was homozygous red. Furthermore, the Hanwoo animals are all yellow
212 coated. The fact that the sweep in Angus was not found using the BovineSNP50 data suggests a
213 resolution issue with the requirement that at least 6 contiguous loci spanning at least 200 kb be
214 fixed in order to declare a sweep. On the other hand, the fact that a sweep was detected in the
215 AFFXB1P data for Simmental that was not detected using the BovineSNP50 data suggests a
216 sampling issue since of the 12 chromosomes representing this breed, only 6 originated in
217 Simmental for this region of the genome. The result for Hanwoo is more interesting since the
218 sweep was declared in Hanwoo using the BovineSNP50 data for 48 individuals but was not
219 confirmed in the 11 individuals genotyped with the AFFXB1P data. This suggests that either the
220 region is not correctly assembled, or that an ancient breed foundation event may have occurred in
221 which the yellow allele was fixed in this breed, but that sufficient mutation events have occurred
222 on this MC1R haplotype to cause it to fail to be detected as a sweep using the high density data.
223 Finally, of particular interest is the fact that no sweep was identified in this genomic region in
224 Wagyu cattle suggesting that black coat color in Angus and Wagyu cattle may not be allelic.
225 Recently, a mutation within (cid:1)-defensin 103 (CBD103) has been shown to cause black coat color
226 in dogs [30]. The cattle ortholog of CBD103 maps to 4.89 Mb on BTA27 centromeric of the
227 sweep that was detected in Wagyu cattle (Table 4).
228 A region on BTA16 at 45.39-45.65 Mb was identified as containing a sweep in six breeds
229 including Angus and Simmental in the BovineSNP50 data and was validated in Angus and
230 Simmental using the AFFXB1P data. This region contains RERE a member of the atrophin
231 family of arginine-glutamic acid dipeptide repeat-containing proteins that is implicated in
232 embryogenesis and embryonic survival in drosophila, mice and zebrafish [31-33]. RERE encodes
233 a tissue-specific transcriptional corepressor, which in association with histone deacetylase,
234 directs growth factor activity and early embryonic patterning [31, 33]. A second sweep was also
235 predicted nearby on BTA16 in Hanwoo and identified at 52.63-52.86 Mb using the AFFXB1P
236 data in Simmental and Wagyu, but not in Hanwoo. However, this 228 kb region includes only 30
237 SNPs in the AFFXB1P assay, a relatively small number of SNPs compared to the 107-321 SNPs
238 in the other four sweep regions validated using the AFFXB1P data. The region contains 11
239 protein coding genes including agrin (AGRN), ISG15 ubiquitin-like modifier, and hairy and
240 enhancer of split 4 in Drosophila (HES4). Two of these genes are involved in embryonic
241 development; AGRN functions in the process of cell adhesion between the embryo and maternal
242 tissues [34] while HES4 is a member of a family of genes known to be oscillators and repressors
243 that affect embryonic developmental events [35]. The ISG15 ubiquitin-like modifier encodes a
244 protein that is present in the uterus of many species, including ruminants, during early pregnancy
245 and is one of the many proteins responsible for acknowledging and maintaining pregnancy [36,
246 37]. The proximity of the two BTA16 sweep regions and involvement of their constituent genes
247 in embryonic development may not be coincidental.
248 For the 66 breed-specific regions identified within the AFFXB1P data (Table 5), many
249 regions contained several genes while a few contained no annotated genes. Five regions
250 contained only one annotated gene or pseudogene as well as some miscellaneous RNAs and
251 uncharacterized protein coding regions. Of these, three sweep regions predicted in Wagyu on
252 BTA1 and BTA27 contained pseudogenes. The sweep region predicted in Wagyu on BTA27
253 contains a pseudogene of chromobox homolog (CBX1) which is a gene involved in the
254 epigenetic reprogramming of the genome during embryo development and later regulates gene
255 expression [38-40]. One sweep region on BTA3 predicted in Angus contains TSPAN2.
256 Tetraspanin-2 is involved in oligodendrocyte signaling and maturation [41, 42]. The fifth
257 predicted sweep region harboring only a single gene was found on BTA28 in Simmental. This
258 region contains adenosine kinase (ADK) which catalyzes phosphate transfer from ATP to
259 adenosine and controls the concentrations of adenine nucleotides both intra- and extra-cellularly.
260 Variation in adenosine abundance affects many physiological processes such as rate of blood
261 flow throughout the body and neurotransmission within the brain [43]. Probably the most
262 interesting gene within this group is found on BTA11in Wagyu. Neurexin 1 (NRXN1) is located
263 in the sweep; mutations in neurexin genes have been theoretically linked to cognitive diseases
264 since they are important in synaptic function [44]. Mutations in human NRXN1 have been
265 confirmed to cause autism spectrum disorders via neurotransmission activity [45]. The link
266 between genes involved in psychiatric disorders poses a potential link to selection based on cattle
267 temperament. Mutations in these genes may confer behavioral changes that are conducive to
268 improved temperament when handled by humans and could have been selected during
269 domestication or more recently following breed formation to develop more manageable animals.
270 When human and porcine orthologs were sought in regions found to contain a single
271 annotated bovine gene, only one region was found to harbor additional loci in human. The sweep
272 region on BTA1 is orthologous to a region on the long arm of HSA9 which contains the iron-
273 sulfur cluster assembly 1 homolog (ISCA1). This gene is important for functionality of a
274 mitochondrial protein in the Fe-S pathway and mutations within ISCA1 cause many conditions
275 and diseases in humans [46]. All of the remaining regions appear to contain only a single gene
276 and these genes are strong candidates for harboring mutations which cause large phenotypic
277 effects that were subjected to strong selective sweeps in these breeds. However, it is also
278 possible that the selected variants are not actually located within coding regions of the genome
279 and are, for example, within non-coding RNAs associated with regulatory genes [24].
280 Among the other detected breed-specific sweep regions, a region on BTA14 specific to
281 Angus cattle harbors several genes including PLAG1 which has been associated with variation in
282 human height [47] and the stature of cattle [48]. Other positional candidates for the stature QTL
283 in this region include RSP20 and SDR16C5 [48]. Of all of the US cattle breeds, Angus has been
284 the most strongly selected for growth and frame size during the last 30 years [49] perhaps
285 creating this selective sweep. The telomeric sweep region on BTA1 found in Angus contains
286 several genes, including LEKR1and CCNL1 in which mutations have been associated with
287 reduced birth weight in humans [50]. Angus cattle have recently been selected to reduce birth
288 weights to ease dystocia and difficulties in birthing and raising calves which are heavier at birth
289 [22]. In Simmental cattle, we found a sweep region on BTA25 that is orthologous to a region on
290 HSA16 which contains genes involved in brain function and neurodevelopment [51-53] that have
291 been associated with autism spectrum disorders. This provides further support for the speculation
292 that cattle have been selected to improve their temperament and docility if we are prepared to
293 accept that mutations in these genes may have less severe behavioral consequences than those
294 associated with autism disorders. Finally, we found three regions harboring selective sweeps in
295 Brahman. A region on BTA10 contains four predicted protein coding domains, none of which
296 are annotated. A second region on BTA22 at 10.70-10.96 Mb contains four genes: TRANK1,
297 DCLK3, GOLGA4, and ITGA9. TRANK1 lies within a region in humans which contains a SNP
298 that is significantly associated with bipolar disorder [54] while doublecortin-like kinase 3 is
299 expressed throughout brain development and in the adult brain [55]. Golgin-4 is the golgin
300 family member that is implicated in providing structure via tethering within the Golgi [56] and
301 integrin alpha 9 is implicated in the reproductive process of gamete interaction. ITGA9 is
302 expressed on the surface of mouse and human eggs and reduced expression decreases the binding
303 ability of the sperm which reduces fertilization success [57]. Once again, the genes located in
304 this region appear to be potentially associated with behavior or reproductive success, a key
305 fitness trait. Perhaps the most interesting of the three selective sweep regions detected in
306 Brahman is located at 48.68-48.90 Mb on BTA5 and contains the methionine sulfoxide reductase
307 B3 (MSRB3) gene which has previously been identified as a candidate for a QTL affecting ear
308 floppiness and morphology in dogs [58, 59]. Brahman cattle were developed in the US as a cross
309 between the Bos taurus indicus breeds Guzerat, Nellore, Gir and Indu Brazil imported primarily
310 from Brazil but all originating in India and the Bos taurus taurus Shorthorn and Hereford breeds
311 [60]. There is considerable variation among these breeds for ear length and morphology with
312 Indu Brazil animals having particularly large, pendulous ears. Thus, the sweep in this region may
313 reflect strong recent selection by breeders to establish a specific Brahman ear morphology type.
314 Of the four regions detected in three or more breeds in the AFFXB1P data, the first on
315 BTA6 was located at 5.64-5.99 Mb which was shared by all four genotyped taurine breeds. It
316 harbors what appears to be a large intron belonging to MAD2L1 which is required for the proper
317 onset of anaphase during cell division. Within the second region on BTA6 at 106.84-107.31 Mb
318 RGS12, a member of the regulator of G protein signaling gene family that directs many cellular
319 activities including hormone signaling, is a positional candidate for an ovulation rate QTL in
320 swine [61, 62]. The detected sweep with a common core at 0.21-0.45 Mb on BTA26 shared by
321 all four taurine breeds contains a series of olfactory receptor loci. The sweep region with a
322 common core at 0.32-0.56 Mb on BTA29 detected in Simmental, Wagyu, and Hanwoo and
323 contains only ncRNA genes ankyrin repeat domain 26 pseudogene 3 (ANKRD26P3) and coiled-
324 coil domain containing 144C (CCDC144C) towards the centromeric end of the region. However
325 towards the center of the predicted sweep region is a region that is highly conserved between
326 human and cow and may represent a long ncRNA. The DAVID functional analysis found no
327 enrichment of ontological terms for the genes located within these selective sweep regions that
328 are shared among multiple breeds and this was expected assuming that each selective sweep was
329 based upon different phenotypes.
330 The presence of olfactory pseudogenes within sweep regions is intriguing and suggests
331 that olfactory loci play a major role in the domesticability of species. Olfactory receptor (OR)
332 genes have previously been found to have been under selection in cattle [12, 25] and more
333 recently in swine [63] and it has been hypothesized that pigs rely intensely on their sense of
334 smell for scavenging. Alterations in the need for wild animals to search for food after their
335 domestication may result in a relaxation of the need for purifying selection acting on these genes
336 allowing them to freely evolve in odorant and tastant detection or lack thereof. In tetrapods,
337 anywhere from 20 to 50% of OR loci exist as pseudogenes [64] and while it is not clear if these
338 genes were ever functional, the acquisition of trichromatic vision has been postulated as
339 facilitating the loss of OR genes [65]. On the other hand, cattle are dichromatic and yet still have
340 a high proportion of OR pseudogenes [64] some of which were found to have been under
341 selection in this study. If these pseudogenes lack functionality, we might have expected them to
342 have been deleted from the genome or to have been significantly disrupted by mutation.
343 However, as many as 67% of OR pseudogenes are expressed in human olfactory epithelium [66]
344 suggesting that similar percentages of bovine OR pseudogenes are also expressed and that many
345 of these loci are functional and rapidly evolving in copy number [25].
346 We utilized two genotyping assays to identify putative selective sweep regions within the
347 bovine genome. The BovineSNP50 assay was employed because we have genotyped a large
348 number of registered animals from several breeds with this assay; however, we recognize that the
349 assay is not ideal for this purpose due to the ascertainment of common SNPs in its design. Since
350 the Bos taurus taurus breeds in Table 1, and Angus and Holstein in particular, were used for
351 SNP discovery and SNPs with high minor allele frequencies in these breeds were preferentially
352 included during the design of the assay [14, 15] it is clearly unsuited to the identification of
353 selective sweep regions that might be common among breeds. However, SNPs were included in
354 the design of the BovineSNP50 assay if they were found to be variable in several, but not
355 necessarily all of these breeds. Therefore, the assay theoretically possesses the ability to identify
356 selective sweeps that are specific to individual breeds or to a small number of breeds. However,
357 rather than characterizing sweeps that occurred during the domestication of cattle and that should
358 therefore be common, e.g., among European taurine breeds that descended from cattle that were
359 domesticated in the Fertile Crescent, these sweeps are much more likely to have occurred during
360 the formation of breeds and will reflect selection to fix phenotypes such as coat color or the
361 absence of horns within specific breeds. A second limitation of this assay is that of calibration
362 relative to the size of the sweep regions. While strong sweeps in numerically small populations
363 are expected to result in the fixation of large haplotypes, weak selection in numerically large
364 populations will result in the fixation of only a small core haplotype which may not be detected
365 using the assay. Thus historic variation in the census population size among breeds may have
366 resulted in variation in the size of the fixed haplotype and the inability to detect small
367 haplotypes. By requiring N contiguous loci to each have a minor allele frequency (MAF) < (cid:1),
368 for small (cid:1), we must choose N to be sufficiently large that it would be highly unlikely to observe
369 N contiguous loci all with a MAF < (cid:1) due to chance alone and yet sufficiently small that the
370 targeted sweeps are not smaller than 37 × (N-1) kb, where 37 kb represents the median
371 intermarker interval on the BovineSNP50 assay. The design of the BovineSNP50 assay also led
372 to lower average MAF and larger numbers of monomorphic loci in breeds such as Brahman, that
373 are phylogenetically distant from the SNP discovery breeds [14]. To adjust for this bias, we
374 defined N separately for each breed (Table 1) requiring larger N for breeds with larger numbers
375 of monomorphic and low MAF loci. The definition of (cid:1)> 0 is also important to this discussion
376 since in the detection of sweeps we must allow for old sweeps in which de novo mutations may
377 have begun to accumulate on the fixed haplotype, genotyping errors which are locus specific but
378 average about 0.5% for this assay, and the incorrect ordering of loci by the UMD3.1 sequence
379 assembly. Errors in the assembly are vastly more likely to cause false negative than false positive
380 sweeps by incorrectly introducing a variable locus into a region of dramatically reduced
381 variability within the genome.
382 We also employed the Affymetrix BOS 1 prescreening assay which contained almost 2.8
383 million putative SNPs that were screened for variability in a small number of animals from
384 several breeds prior to the design of the commercial BOS 1 Axiom assay. While we had many
385 fewer animals genotyped with this assay which influenced the estimation of MAF, the AFFXB1P
386 assay had over 50× the number of SNPs present on the BovineSNP50 assay which offered
387 considerably greater power for identifying small sweeps and the application of this assay also
388 suffers less from ascertainment bias. While loci that have been fixed in all domesticated cattle
389 relative to their auroch forbears will still not appear on this assay due to the requirement that the
390 putative SNP must have been predicted to have been variable in the sequence data for at least
391 one breed, there was much less selection for SNPs with high MAF in numerous breeds in the
392 design of this assay relative to the BovineSNP50. Consequently, we expected this assay to
393 identify putative sweep regions that could not be identified by the application of the
394 BovineSNP50 assay and to more precisely define the boundaries of sweeps that were detected by
395 the BovineSNP50 assay and validated by the AFFXB1P assay.
396 We found evidence for selective sweeps in genomic regions that were detected to have
397 diverged between breeds using integrated haplotype scores (iHS) and F statistics in the Bovine
398 HapMap project [12]. Putative sweep regions overlapped on chromosomes 2, 11, 12, and 14
399 detected either by an extreme F or iHS value. We found evidence for only one selective sweep
400 region for which the Bovine HapMap project [11] identified divergence between breeds using
401 F statistics. Using the BovineSNP50 data, we found a putative sweep in Angus, Salers,
402 Shorthorn and Simmental cattle on BTA12 in a region harboring NBEA that was found to have
403 differentiated among similar taurine breeds. The fact that only one such region was detected
404 probably reflects the fact that our approach requires a completed sweep to have occurred whereas
405 use of the F statistic can detect an ongoing sweep for which breeds have diverged.
406 No putative selective sweep regions were found in common between Brahman and any of
407 the B. t. taurus breeds which likely reflects the recent admixture that occurred in the formation of
408 the Brahman and the fact that the breed does not share a common phenotype such as coat color
409 with any of the taurine breeds. Furthermore, indicine cattle are more commonly found in the
410 southern tier of the US where they are exposed to higher temperatures and humidities and lower
411 pasture qualities and availability than are taurine cattle which are more frequently found in the
412 northern US. Consequently, we would not expect these breeds to have been subjected to
413 selection for common morphological or adaptive phenotypes. Additionally, no common sweeps
414 were detected between the cattle sub-species possibly due to the more severe ascertainment bias
415 on MAF for BovineSNP50 loci in Brahman cattle. While SNP discovery was performed in
416 Brahman for the development of the AFFXB1P assay, the number of indicine breeds sequenced
417 for SNP discovery was small relative to the number of sequenced taurine breeds leading to a bias
418 towards SNPs common in taurine cattle being included on the assay. However, the density of
419 SNPs on this assay is so great (~1 SNP / kb) that we did not expect the reliability of sweep
420 regions identified in Brahman to be significantly less than those identified in taurine breeds. Our
421 identification of a putative sweep region harboring a previously identified QTL for ear length
422 and floppiness in Brahman is consistent with the introduction of the undesirable allele from Indu
423 Brazil cattle during breed formation and subsequent strong selection by breeders to remove the
424 allele and fix a shorter ear type within the breed.
425 Low density assays, such as the BovineSNP50 assay, have previously been utilized to
426 identify runs of homozygosity (ROH) within cattle breeds. The BovineSNP50 assay was found
427 to be adequate for finding ROH and estimating inbreeding coefficients in cattle [67]. However,
428 this is not the case for the detection of selective sweeps which typically span smaller regions of
429 the genome than ROH which are frequently due to consanguinity and which may represent as
430 much as 12.39% of the genome [67]. The use of low density SNP data for the detection of
431 selective sweeps appears to be inadequate primarily due to the poor calibration of SNP density
432 relative to the size of the targeted sweep regions such that any relaxation of the number of
433 contiguous SNPs with fixed alleles required to detect smaller sweep regions leads to an elevated
434 type I error rate. Strong, recent selective sweeps causing the fixation of large haplotypes may be
435 identified using low-density SNP panels (~50,000 SNPs), however, older sweeps which have
436 accumulated new mutations and weak sweeps which have resulted in the fixation of relatively
437 small haplotypes will be missed.
438 Several putative selective sweeps identified using the BovineSNP50 assay failed to be
439 validated using the AFFXB1P assay. While many fewer animals representing each breed were
440 genotyped using the AFFXB1P assay, among the 50× additional SNPs within each such region,
441 we found that at least 5% of the SNP had a MAF > (2M)-1 where M is the number of genotyped
442 animals. We have previously found that the genotyping error rate of loci on the Affymetrix
443 Axiom BOS 1 assay is very similar to that of the Illumina BovineSNP50 and HD assays (~0.5%,
444 data not shown) and thus, we do not expect genotyping errors to explain this result, although it is
445 certainly a possibility. It appears that the phenomenon is random and associated with the number
446 of regions expected to meet the criteria for declaring a sweep when ~8,000 of the 54,001
447 BovineSNP50 loci are monomorphic within a breed (Table 1) [68, 69]. For example, if 15% of
448 SNPs are monomorphic within a breed, the probability that N contiguous SNPs are
449 monomorphic is 0.15N, assuming independence, and in testing 50,000 SNP on 29 autosomes we
450 would expect to find 0.15N × (50,000 – 29 × (N – 1)) regions in which N contiguous SNPs had
451 fixed alleles. For N = 5 this corresponds to 4 false positives per breed and 0.6 false positives per
452 breed when N = 6 (Table 1). As a consequence, the reliability of declaration of a selective sweep
453 is dramatically improved when sweeps are found to be common between breeds, particularly
454 when the breeds are phylogenetically distant. We found several sweep regions that were common
455 to two or more breeds and five predicted sweeps detected by the BovineSNP50 assay were
456 validated by the AFFXB1P data.
457 Identifying the mutations that underlie these sweep regions will be paramount to more
458 fully understanding the effects of human interaction on the genomes of domesticated cattle.
459 Candidates will soon become available by sequencing the genomes of individuals that are
460 homozygous for identical SNP haplotypes within a sweep region but where some originate from
461 the breeds predicted to have undergone a selective sweep and the others from breeds in which no
462 sweep was detected. We found many regions to contain pseudogenes and protein coding regions
463 but others that contained no annotated genes. While this reflects the nascent state of annotation
464 of the bovine genome, it also reflects the fact that the mutations we seek may not be protein
465 coding. For example, the mutation within the sweep region on BTA1 in Angus cattle that
466 removes horns from these animals appears to be a ncRNA [6]. However, even after these
467 mutations have been identified, our understanding of the phenotype that was created and selected
468 to complete fixation may still be limited. The functional analyses of the genes within the selected
469 regions sheds little light on this since each region likely harbored variants which influence
470 different phenotypes. Finally, while our sampling of breeds was small, we found little evidence
471 for the sharing of sweeps among phylogenetically closely related breeds. This suggests that the
472 sweeps are mostly recent, post-dating breed formation and occurred for a variety of phenotypes
473 for which selective value differed among the humans who developed these breeds.
474 Conclusions
476 We identified selective sweeps that appear to primarily have been due to breed formation events.
477 Due to the constraint that SNPs be variable in multiple breeds imposed during the design of the
478 utilized assays, we did not identify any sweeps that were common to all breeds within the study.
479 There were also no sweep regions predicted to be in common between breeds of taurine and
480 indicine descent probably reflecting the different environmental and demographic forces to
481 which these sub-species have been exposed during breed formation. For several of the detected
482 regions we are able to identify the phenotypes and genes that were subjected to selection or to
483 propose these based upon the results of previous mapping studies. However, for many of these
484 regions the selected gene and phenotype are unclear. The fact that so many of the detected sweep
485 regions harbor genes associated with reproductive processes or embryonic development is
486 probably not remarkable considering the fact that strong selection acts on these fitness traits and
487 that the time required to achieve fixation of variants of modest effect may be considerably longer
488 than the 200 years since breed formation during which strong human selection has acted.
489 We demonstrate that the resolution and SNP ascertainment bias inherent in the design of
490 the assay used to detect selective sweeps is of paramount importance and that the BovineSNP50
491 assay is not generally suitable for this purpose due to the high type I error rates that are likely to
492 be encountered. SNP ascertainment bias leads to lower MAF in breeds that are phylogenetically
493 distant from the SNP discovery breeds and an increased rate of monomorphic SNPs with these
494 breeds. As whole genome sequencing becomes less expensive these problems will likely be
495 ameliorated by sequencing a few distantly related individuals from each breed and this approach
496 may also be used to identify candidate mutations underlying each sweep. However, the approach
497 is reliant on the alignment of sequences to a Hereford reference assembly [25] which introduces
498 a new set of biases unless de novo sequence assemblies can accurately be created for each breed.
499 The identification of genes and variants underlying historical selective sweeps is of
500 interest from the perspective of understanding how human interaction with cattle has influenced
501 the patterning of variation within the bovine genome. Perhaps of more importance, the discovery
502 of the selected variants will lead to the identification of large effect QTLs and ultimately a better
503 understanding as to the phenotypes which are affected by variation within genes and regulatory
504 elements.
506 Materials and Methods
507 Samples, design, and genotyping
508 We utilized two data sets comprising high-density SNPs scored in animals that were registered
509 by their respective breed societies. The first data set comprised 6,373 animals from 13 taurine
510 breeds including Angus, Braunvieh, Charolais, Hanwoo, Hereford, Limousin, Salers, Shorthorn,
511 Simmental, Brown Swiss, Finnish Ayrshire, Holstein, and Jersey, and the indicine Brahman
512 breed (Table 1). Genotypes scored in these animals were generated using the Illumina
513 BovineSNP50 BeadChip which assayed 54,001 loci with a median intermarker interval of 37 kb
514 [68, 69]. The second data set which was used both for the discovery of putative selective sweep
515 regions and for validation of results obtained from the analysis of the BovineSNP50 data set
516 comprised 58 animals from the Angus, Hanwoo, Simmental, Wagyu, and Brahman breeds (Table
517 2) which were genotyped with the Affymetrix prescreening assay comprising 2,787,037 SNPs
518 with a median intermarker interval of 975 bp that was used to generate allele frequency estimates
519 for the design of the Axiom Genome-Wide BOS 1 assay [70].
520 The sampled breeds were chosen based on their geographical origins, historical uses by
521 human, diverse phylogenetic relationships and because of the availability of at least 40
522 BovineSNP50 genotyped individuals. Each BovineSNP50 genotyped individual was registered
523 with its respective breed association and was proven by pedigree-analysis to be purebred, since
524 some associations (e.g., Simmental, Limousin) allow the registration of crossbred cattle. This
525 sampling strategy was employed to ensure that there would be minimal effects of recent
526 introgression between the breeds following breed formation.
527 SNP filtering
528 All X-linked loci were removed from the analysis due to the greater number of assembly issues
529 that are associated with this chromosome and also because the studied animals were male
530 resulting in a halving of the number of chromosomes sampled for each breed which leads to a
531 reduction in the precision of allele frequency estimation. The remaining BovineSNP50 genotypes
532 were filtered on call rate < 85% which left a total of 52,942 SNPs. Within the higher density
533 AFFXB1P data, we required SNPs to have a call rate of ≥85% across all 5 breeds and a
534 minimum call rate of 50% within each of the individual breeds. Following filtering 2,575,339
535 SNPs remained.
536 Identification of putative selective sweep regions
537 The BovineSNP50 data were analyzed by breed to identify putative selective sweeps. Because
538 the number of variable loci differed within each breed primarily due to the breed of origin of
539 SNP discovery in the design of the assay [14, 15], we required a breed-specific number (Table
540 1), and at a minimum 5, contiguous SNPs spanning at least 200 kb based upon UMD3.1
541 coordinates for which no SNP had a minor allele frequency MAF > 0.01 to declare a selective
542 sweep. Following the breed-specific analysis, an across-breed comparison was performed in
543 which any putative selective sweep which overlapped in two or more of the breeds was
544 considered a common sweep and the selected haplotypes present within each breed were
545 identified.
546 Validation of selective sweeps
547 To validate the putative sweep regions detected using the BovineSNP50 data, we used a high-
548 density data set representing SNPs genotyped at approximately a 50-fold increase in resolution,
549 but scored in only a small subsample from 5 breeds. However, the analysis performed with these
550 data was not guided by the results of the analysis of the BovineSNP50 data. We independently
551 analyzed the SNPs as conducted for the BovineSNP50 data to identify putative selective sweeps
552 and then compared the results of the analysis of the AFFXB1P data to those from the analyses of
553 the BovineSNP50 data to identify putative selective sweeps identified within each breed by both
554 analyses. To declare a putative selective sweep region we required the region to harbor at least
555 20 contiguous SNPs spanning at least 100 kb, with no more than 5% of the SNP having a MAF >
556 (2N)-1 where N was the number of individuals with genotypes for the SNP within the analyzed
557 breed. Among the variable SNPs, we further required that no more than 3 be contiguous. These
558 thresholds were set to allow the detection of no more than one heterozygous individual for each
559 variable SNP within a selective sweep region to allow for genotyping errors and for new
560 variation to have been created within each region by mutation. These conditions also allow for
561 the possibility that the SNPs may not have been correctly ordered by the UMD3.1 assembly and
562 variable contigs may have been erroneously included within scaffolds containing a selective
563 sweep.
564 Annotation and functional analysis
565 Annotation of the genes present within all putative selective sweep regions was performed using
566 the UCSC Genome Browser [71] and NCBI Gene database. Genes for which annotations were
567 retrieved included any genes that were fully or partially contained within each region.
568 Phenotypes known to be affected by variation in these genes were determined from a search of
569 the literature and were assessed for their likely causality for each sweep. Functional analyses
570 were performed for the sweeps detected within each breed using the functional annotation and
571 clustering tools in the Database for Annotation, Visualization and Integrated Discovery
572 (DAVID) [72].
